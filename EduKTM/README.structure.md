Below is a **China-focused research and implementation overview of digital education**, with emphasis on developments from **2022–2026**, especially the rapid shift from online education toward **AI-enabled “smart education.”**

## 1. Executive summary

China has moved through three broad phases:

1. **Digitization / online access** — digitizing educational resources and expanding connectivity.
2. **Platformization** — building a national public digital-education infrastructure around the **Smart Education of China (SEC) platform**.
3. **Intelligentization** — integrating generative AI, educational large language models, learning analytics, intelligent tutors, AI literacy, and data-driven governance.

The important point is that China is no longer treating digital education simply as **“online classes.”** Its current policy framework treats digital technology as infrastructure for changing **curriculum, pedagogy, assessment, teacher development, educational administration, lifelong learning, and education governance**.

The scale is substantial. By April 2025, the national Smart Education of China platform reportedly had **more than 164 million registered users, 61.3 billion visits, more than 110,000 basic-education resources, 11,300+ vocational courses, 31,000 higher-education courses, and 2,000+ lifelong-learning resources**. ([Gov.cn][1])

And by 2026, China's policy agenda had moved explicitly toward **“AI + Education.”** A five-ministry action plan released in April 2026 calls for AI education throughout the school system, AI as a university foundational subject, AI-supported personalized learning, intelligent teaching assistants, AI-enabled educational governance, and a national AI-education ecosystem. ([Ministry of Education][2])

# 3. The major policy transition

The policy trajectory is particularly important for understanding research and implementation.

### 2022: National platformization

The launch of the national Smart Education of China platform marked a shift from fragmented digital resources toward a national public infrastructure.

### 2023–2024: Large-scale application

The focus increasingly became:

> **How do we make digital education part of ordinary teaching rather than merely provide online resources?**

The Ministry of Education introduced large-scale application pilots, including Guangdong and Hainan, as well as pilots in western and ethnic-minority regions. ([Ministry of Education][5])

### 2024: Integration + intelligence + internationalization

China formally emphasized three directions:

* **integrated**
* **intelligent**
* **internationalized**

Digital resources were increasingly connected to teaching, teacher training, research, school administration and educational services. ([Ministry of Education][6])

### 2025: AI becomes central

A major April 2025 policy document from nine ministries called for:

* AI educational large models
* multimodal educational datasets
* knowledge graphs
* competency graphs
* intelligent textbooks
* AI-supported curricula
* AI tutors
* digital mentors
* personalized learning
* intelligent student communities
* AI-enabled educational decision-making
* education data infrastructure

It explicitly calls for AI to be integrated into the **whole educational process**, rather than treated as an isolated technology. ([Government of China][7])

### 2026: “AI + Education”

The April 2026 action plan represents another step toward institutionalization.

China's stated 2030 objective is to establish a relatively mature AI–education integration framework covering:

* all educational stages
* AI literacy
* AI talent development
* teaching
* research
* educational governance
* infrastructure
* innovation ecosystems

The plan specifically calls for AI to become a **university foundational course** and for AI education to become progressively embedded in primary and secondary curricula. ([Ministry of Education][2])

# 4. The five major implementation areas

## A. K–12 education

Digital education in Chinese primary and secondary schools now goes beyond recorded lessons.

The national platform supports:

* digital textbooks/resources
* online lessons
* teacher preparation
* online teaching research
* homework
* classroom management
* assessment
* AI learning assistance
* remote teaching
* inter-school resource sharing

The 2024 national pilot specifically targeted:

**Guangdong + Hainan**

and

**Tibet + Qinghai + Ningxia + Xinjiang**

while encouraging eastern provinces such as Beijing, Shanghai and Jiangsu to share high-quality resources. ([Ministry of Education][8])

### Rural–urban model

One particularly important implementation model is the **“cloud classroom.”**

A rural school can connect to a higher-quality urban school, allowing:

> urban teacher + rural teacher + rural students

to participate in the same instructional environment.

The Ministry reports examples of rural schools pairing with top urban schools through the national platform. ([Ministry of Education][9])

This is strategically important because China's digital-education policy explicitly tries to use technology to address **regional educational inequality**, rather than simply improve already advantaged schools.

# 5. Vocational education

Vocational education is another major application area.

China has used digital platforms for:

* technical training
* industrial skills
* simulation
* online courses
* virtual laboratories
* professional certification
* industry–education collaboration
* workforce reskilling

The national platform had more than **11,300 vocational online courses** by April 2025. ([Gov.cn][1])

A particularly interesting direction is the combination of:

**digital education + virtual simulation + industrial training**

For example, Zhejiang has used virtual simulation technology to create immersive learning environments connecting school education with industrial training. ([Ministry of Education][9])

This matters because China's digital-education strategy is closely connected to workforce development and industrial upgrading.

# 6. Higher education and MOOCs

China has developed an enormous university MOOC ecosystem.

The national platform reportedly contained around:

**31,000 higher-education courses**

by 2025. ([Gov.cn][1])

Another major initiative is:

### MOOCs for Western China

The program attempts to use digital courses to compensate for disparities in university resources between eastern and western China.

According to the Ministry of Education, since its launch in 2013 the program had provided approximately:

* **207,000 online courses**
* **9.36 million joint teaching activities**
* benefits to approximately **590 million student participations**
* professional-development support for nearly **2 million teachers**

by 2024. ([Ministry of Education][10])

The numbers are best interpreted as **participations/interactions**, not unique students.

# 7. Lifelong learning

This is one of the most interesting aspects of China's strategy.

China increasingly sees digital education as infrastructure for a:

> **“anyone, anywhere, anytime” learning society**

rather than merely school education.

The national platform has therefore expanded into:

* professional training
* vocational skills
* adult education
* continuing education
* cultural learning
* language learning
* retirement/elder learning
* personal development

The 2026 upgrade added a dedicated **lifelong-learning center**, with vocational skills, knowledge upgrading, cultural/social learning and leisure education. ([Ministry of Education][11])

This reflects a broader policy objective: connecting education with a rapidly changing labor market.

# 9. Important recent academic research

Here are some papers particularly useful for understanding the evidence base.

## 9.1 Effectiveness of EdTech in China

**“Generalizable evidence that computer assisted learning improves student learning: A systematic review of education technology in China” — Computers and Education Open, 2024**

This is one of the most useful evidence syntheses.

The authors reviewed **18 unique studies / 21 manuscripts** involving Chinese primary and secondary education.

The pooled effect was approximately:

**+0.13 standard deviations**

for student learning outcomes.

The review found generally positive results for computer-assisted learning used as a supplement to existing educational inputs, and positive evidence for remote instruction, although the evidence base remains limited. ([ScienceDirect][12])

### Important qualification

This does **not** mean “technology improves education by 0.13 SD everywhere.”

The effect varies with:

* implementation
* teacher involvement
* student characteristics
* technology type
* subject
* learning context

That distinction is important when interpreting China's very large implementation numbers.

---

# 10. A crucial counterpoint: technology does not automatically improve outcomes

One of the most valuable Chinese studies is:

**“Isolating the ‘Tech’ from EdTech: Experimental Evidence on Computer-Assisted Learning in China” — Economic Development and Cultural Change, 2024**

This study used a field experiment in rural China to separate the effect of the **technology itself** from other components of EdTech programs.

The authors found **null effects on mathematics test scores** for both the overall CAL program and the technology-specific effect in their experiment. ([ScienceDirect][13])

This is extremely important.

It suggests that:

> **Digital technology is not itself the causal mechanism of better learning.**

Teacher support, instructional design, time-on-task, motivation and implementation quality can matter as much as the software.

---

# 11. Digital education and inequality

This is probably the most important critical research area.

A 2024 study:

**“Does online education magnify educational inequalities? Evidence from the COVID-19 pandemic”**

used China's COVID school closures as a quasi-natural experiment.

The study found:

* stronger-performing students benefited more
* previously low-performing students could be negatively affected
* students from higher-SES families improved more
* the academic gap increased under prolonged distance learning

The researchers estimate a **16.7% widening of the performance gap** between disadvantaged and higher-SES students under the studied conditions. ([ScienceDirect][14])

So there is a fundamental tension:

**Digital education can democratize access to high-quality content while simultaneously amplifying differences in students' ability to use that content effectively.**

---

# 12. The “three digital divides”

Another important 2024 study of **3,961 Chinese university students** examined rural–urban digital inequality.

It identified:

### First digital divide

**Access**

Differences in:

* Internet quality
* device performance
* cost
* connectivity

### Second digital divide

**Skills**

Differences in students' ability to use digital technologies.

### Third digital divide

**Knowledge absorption**

Even when students have equivalent access and technical skills, they may differ in their ability to transform digital resources into actual learning.

([Taylor & Francis Online][15])

This third divide is especially important for the next generation of Chinese digital-education research.

---

# 13. Online-learning adaptation

A 2024 large-scale study of Chinese universities examined what makes students adapt to online learning.

One significant finding was that:

**self-regulated learning ability can be more important than teacher e-readiness in determining students' intention to continue online learning.**

Teacher digital readiness still matters, but students' ability to regulate their own learning can partially substitute for external instructional support. ([Springer Link][16])

This suggests that effective digital education requires teaching students **how to learn digitally**, not simply giving them digital content.

---

# 14. Generative AI research in Chinese universities

A 2025 study of Chinese university students examined perceptions, adoption intentions and impacts of generative AI. ([Springer Link][17])

Another 2025 study surveyed **148 Chinese engineering students** regarding generative AI.

More than half reported improvements in:

* learning efficiency
* initiative
* creativity

and nearly half believed AI improved independent thinking.

However, many students did **not** perceive a corresponding improvement in actual academic performance and expressed concerns about:

* factual accuracy
* domain-specific reliability
* overdependence

([arXiv][18])

This is consistent with a broader finding in international AI-education research:

> **Students' perceived productivity gains are easier to demonstrate than durable learning gains.**

# 15. Generative AI policy in Chinese universities

A 2025 cross-national study examined **124 policy documents from 110 universities** in China, Japan and the United States.

The study characterized Chinese universities as operating within a relatively **centralized, government-led policy environment**, with strong emphasis on technology application and active exploration of generative-AI integration. ([arXiv][19])

This is significant because China is developing two things simultaneously:

### Technology adoption

Universities are rapidly introducing:

* LLMs
* AI courses
* AI research
* AI teaching assistants
* AI laboratories

### Governance

Institutions must also address:

* academic integrity
* privacy
* data security
* model reliability
* intellectual property
* appropriate student use

In December 2024, China's Ministry of Education issued guidance for AI education in primary and secondary schools.

The progression is broadly:

| Stage            | Emphasis                           |
| ---------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| Lower primary    | Awareness of AI                    |
| Upper primary    | Understanding and using AI         |
| Junior secondary | AI use and understanding           |
| Senior secondary | Projects and advanced applications |

The guidance also encourages:

* project-based learning
* AI laboratories
* university–school cooperation
* industry participation
* teacher training
* online cooperation between urban and rural schools

([Ministry of Education][20])

So China's model is evolving from:

**“students use digital technology”**

toward:

**“students understand, create with and critically evaluate AI.”**

# 20. Teacher development

China's strategy recognizes a major bottleneck:

> **Teachers must change along with the technology.**

The national platform has therefore become a teacher-development system as well as a student-learning system.

By the end of 2025, official figures reported that the platform had created more than **500,000 teacher-research groups** and supported more than **90 million teacher-training participations**. ([Ministry of Education][24])

The important research question here is not simply whether teachers have access to technology.

It is:

**Does digital professional development change classroom practice?**

This remains considerably less well established than the evidence for platform adoption.

# 21. Data governance and ethics

As China's digital education system becomes increasingly data-intensive, governance becomes a central research area.

The 2025 policy explicitly addresses:

* educational-data security
* personal-information protection
* algorithm security
* AI-model governance
* data lifecycle protection
* algorithm registration
* AI safety assessment
* risks of overdependence
* algorithmic bias
* information bubbles

([Government of China][7])

In May 2026, China also released an **“Ethics of Digital Education: A Reference Framework.”**

It emphasizes:

1. Human decision-making
2. Human–AI complementarity
3. Context-specific AI deployment
4. differentiated governance according to risk

It also categorizes AI applications into **prohibited, limited and encouraged** scenarios. ([Ministry of Education][25])

This is a significant development because AI governance is becoming part of the education architecture rather than an afterthought.

# 22. What the evidence currently tells us

A useful synthesis of the research is:

| Question                                            | Current evidence                               |
| --------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| Does digital education improve access?              | **Strong evidence**                            |
| Can it expand access to high-quality resources?     | **Strong evidence**                            |
| Can online learning improve learning outcomes?      | **Generally positive, but heterogeneous**      |
| Does technology itself cause better learning?       | **Not necessarily**                            |
| Can digital education reduce geographic inequality? | **Potentially, but implementation matters**    |
| Can it worsen socioeconomic inequality?             | **Yes, under some conditions**                 |
| Does AI increase productivity?                      | **Strong emerging evidence**                   |
| Does AI reliably improve learning outcomes?         | **Still uncertain**                            |
| Can AI personalize education?                       | **Technically increasingly feasible**          |
| Does AI personalization improve long-term learning? | **Insufficient longitudinal evidence**         |
| Does AI reduce teacher workload?                    | **Promising but insufficient causal evidence** |
| Does AI create academic-integrity problems?         | **Clearly yes**                                |
| Is teacher digital literacy important?              | **Yes**                                        |
| Is student self-regulation important?               | **Very important**                             |
| Does platform scale guarantee educational quality?  | **No**                                         |

# 23. The central contradiction in China's digital-education model

There is a very important distinction between:

**digital resource equality**

and

**educational outcome equality**.

China has been extremely successful at the first.

A rural student can potentially access the same digital lesson as a student in Beijing or Shanghai.

But that does not guarantee the same outcome because students differ in:

* home environment
* parental education
* digital literacy
* teacher support
* motivation
* self-regulation
* device quality
* Internet quality
* prior achievement

The academic literature increasingly supports this distinction. ([ScienceDirect][14])

Therefore, the next generation of Chinese digital-education policy is increasingly concerned with **“deep application”**, rather than simply increasing platform usage.

# 24. The most important research directions for 2026–2030

Based on the current policy and research trajectory, I would divide China's research agenda into approximately eight areas.

### 1. AI tutors and intelligent agents

Research questions:

* Can AI provide genuinely adaptive instruction?
* How much teacher intervention is necessary?
* Can AI identify misconceptions?
* Can AI generate individualized learning paths?

### 2. Human–AI teacher collaboration

Instead of:

> AI replaces teacher

the emerging model is:

> **Teacher + AI + student**

Research will examine which tasks should remain human and which can be automated.

### 3. AI assessment

This could become particularly important in China because of its examination-oriented education system.

Potential applications include:

* automated formative assessment
* adaptive testing
* competency diagnosis
* writing assessment
* oral-language assessment
* mathematical reasoning analysis

### 4. AI-resistant assessment

Universities will need new assessment models because traditional take-home assignments become increasingly vulnerable to generative AI.

Potential approaches:

* oral defense
* project-based assessment
* process portfolios
* classroom assessment
* personalized questioning
* AI-use disclosure

### 5. Educational data infrastructure

China is increasingly building national educational data infrastructure.

Research questions include:

* interoperability
* data standards
* privacy
* algorithmic fairness
* predictive analytics
* longitudinal student records

### 6. Rural and regional inequality

The key question is shifting from:

> “Does the rural school have Internet?”

to:

> **“Can rural students obtain the same educational benefit from digital infrastructure?”**

### 7. Lifelong learning

Digital education will increasingly connect:

**school → university → vocational training → employment → reskilling → retirement learning**

This is especially important as China's demographic structure and labor market change.

### 8. AI literacy

AI literacy will probably become as fundamental as conventional digital literacy.

Students will need to understand:

* how AI works
* how to use AI
* how to verify AI output
* bias
* hallucination
* privacy
* copyright
* academic integrity
* human–AI collaboration

# 27. Overall assessment of China's position

The most accurate way to characterize China's digital education development is **not simply “China is using a lot of EdTech.”**

China is attempting something considerably larger:

> **a national digital education infrastructure in which educational resources, teaching, learning, teachers, AI, educational data, public services, vocational development and lifelong learning are increasingly integrated into one ecosystem.**

The strongest part of the Chinese model is its **scale and centralized infrastructure**. The national platform makes resource aggregation and nationwide deployment unusually feasible. Official figures show extremely large levels of platform participation. ([Gov.cn][1])

The major unresolved issue is **conversion of technological scale into durable educational outcomes**.

Academic evidence suggests that digital tools can improve learning, but effects are generally modest and highly dependent on implementation. At the same time, online education can increase inequality when students have different socioeconomic resources, digital skills or self-regulation capabilities. ([ScienceDirect][12])

The next stage is therefore likely to be less about:

**“How much digital education does China have?”**

and more about:

**“Can AI and digital infrastructure produce measurable improvements in learning quality, equity, teacher productivity and lifelong human capability?”**

That is the central research question for China's **2026–2030 digital-education phase**.

### A useful conceptual model

**2022–23:**
Digital resources → national platform

**2024:**
National platform → large-scale classroom application

**2025:**
Digital education → AI-enabled education

**2026 onward:**
AI + education → personalized learning + intelligent teaching + data governance + lifelong learning

([Ministry of Education][29])
