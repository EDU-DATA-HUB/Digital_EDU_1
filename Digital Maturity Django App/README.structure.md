Question
Methods for evaluating the level of digitalization in education

Answer
There are several established ways to evaluate the **level of digitalization in education**, but no single metric captures it adequately. A robust assessment usually combines **digital infrastructure, technology adoption, pedagogical transformation, digital competencies, learning outcomes, governance, and equity**.

## 1. A multidimensional evaluation framework

A useful framework is to evaluate digitalization across **six dimensions**:

| Dimension                         | What to measure                                  | Typical indicators                                                                  |
| --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **1. Digital infrastructure**     | Availability and quality of technology           | Internet bandwidth, device/student ratio, Wi-Fi coverage, cloud infrastructure      |
| **2. Technology adoption**        | How extensively digital tools are actually used  | LMS penetration, online course usage, digital content use, platform usage frequency |
| **3. Pedagogical digitalization** | Whether technology changes teaching and learning | Blended learning, flipped classrooms, adaptive learning, collaborative platforms    |
| **4. Digital competence**         | Capabilities of teachers and students            | Digital literacy, ICT skills, AI literacy, information literacy                     |
| **5. Digital learning outcomes**  | Effects on learning and teaching                 | Achievement, engagement, retention, personalized learning, feedback quality         |
| **6. Governance & equity**        | Institutional capacity and inclusiveness         | Digital strategy, data governance, cybersecurity, accessibility, digital divide     |

This prevents a common problem: **equating "having computers and Internet" with being digitally transformed**.

---

# 2. Maturity-level models

Another common method is a **digital maturity model**.

For example, an institution can be classified into five stages:

### Level 1 — Initial

* Limited computers and connectivity
* Digital tools used occasionally
* Mostly paper-based teaching
* Little institutional strategy

### Level 2 — Digitized

* Basic Internet and devices available
* Administrative processes increasingly digital
* Teachers use presentations, digital textbooks, LMS, etc.

### Level 3 — Integrated

* Digital platforms integrated into teaching
* Blended learning becomes normal
* Student learning data are collected
* Teachers receive systematic digital training

### Level 4 — Data-driven

* Learning analytics
* Adaptive/personalized learning
* AI-assisted teaching
* Data-informed curriculum and intervention

### Level 5 — Transformative

* Digital technology fundamentally changes the educational model
* AI and analytics are embedded into learning
* Highly personalized learning pathways
* Continuous learning ecosystems
* Digital credentials and cross-institutional learning become possible

This type of model is particularly useful for **comparing schools, universities, regions, or countries**.

---

# 3. Indicator-based Digitalization Index

A more quantitative approach is to construct a **Digital Education Index (DEI)**.

For example:

$$
DEI = w_1I + w_2A + w_3P + w_4C + w_5O + w_6G
$$

where:

* \(I\) = infrastructure
* \(A\) = technology adoption
* \(P\) = pedagogical transformation
* \(C\) = digital competence
* \(O\) = educational outcomes
* \(G\) = governance/equity
* \(w_i\) = weights assigned to each dimension

A simple equal-weight model could be:

$$
DEI = \frac{I+A+P+C+O+G}{6}
$$

Each dimension could be scored from **0–100**.

### Example

| Dimension                  |    Score |
| -------------------------- | -------: |
| Infrastructure             |       90 |
| Technology adoption        |       75 |
| Pedagogical transformation |       60 |
| Digital competence         |       70 |
| Learning outcomes          |       65 |
| Governance & equity        |       55 |
| **Overall DEI**            | **69.2** |

The important point is that the overall score should **not hide weaknesses in individual dimensions**.

---

# 4. Process-based evaluation

Instead of simply measuring technology availability, researchers can examine the **educational process**:

**Input → Process → Output → Outcome → Impact**

### Input

* Devices
* Broadband
* Software
* Digital content
* IT staff
* Funding

### Process

* Frequency of LMS use
* Teacher technology adoption
* Student interaction
* Digital assessment
* AI usage
* Data analytics

### Output

* Number of digital courses
* Digital learning materials
* Online assessments
* Digital credentials

### Outcome

* Student achievement
* Engagement
* Digital competencies
* Teacher productivity

### Long-term impact

* Employability
* Educational equality
* Cost efficiency
* Innovation
* Lifelong learning

This is especially useful for **academic research**, because it separates investment in digitalization from its actual educational consequences.

---

# 5. Technology-adoption models

Researchers can also evaluate **how deeply a technology is used**.

One useful conceptual progression is:

**Substitution → Augmentation → Modification → Redefinition**

This is similar to the SAMR framework.

For example:

### Substitution

A paper textbook becomes a PDF.

### Augmentation

Students use an interactive digital textbook.

### Modification

Students collaboratively create multimedia projects online.

### Redefinition

AI/adaptive systems create individualized learning pathways that would be difficult to implement without digital technology.

The distinction is important because two schools may both report **"100% digital textbook adoption"**, while the actual pedagogical transformation may be dramatically different.

---

# 6. Teacher digital competence

A separate assessment should measure teachers.

Possible indicators include:

* Basic ICT competence
* Digital content creation
* LMS competence
* Online assessment
* Digital collaboration
* Learning analytics
* Cybersecurity awareness
* AI literacy
* Digital pedagogy
* Ability to evaluate digital resources

A questionnaire can use a **Likert scale**, e.g.:

> 1 = Never / no competence
> 2 = Basic
> 3 = Intermediate
> 4 = Advanced
> 5 = Expert

However, self-reported competence should ideally be combined with **performance-based assessments**, because people tend to overestimate their digital skills.

---

# 7. Student digitalization assessment

Students can be evaluated across:

### Technical skills

Can the student operate digital tools?

### Information literacy

Can they locate, evaluate, and verify information?

### Communication

Can they collaborate effectively online?

### Content creation

Can they create digital products?

### Computational/AI literacy

Can they understand and appropriately use AI and computational tools?

### Digital citizenship

Can they manage privacy, security, ethics, and responsible online behavior?

This is increasingly important because **access to technology does not necessarily mean digital competence**.

---

# 8. Learning analytics

At universities and large school systems, actual platform data can provide more objective measurements.

Examples:

* LMS login frequency
* Time spent learning
* Assignment submission rates
* Digital resource access
* Discussion participation
* Assessment attempts
* Course completion
* Dropout patterns
* Learning progression
* Use of AI tutoring systems

For example:

$$
Digital\ Engagement =
\frac{\text{active digital learning days}}
{\text{total learning days}}
\times 100
$$

This can provide a more realistic measure than asking students whether they "use digital technology."

Privacy, consent, data minimization, and governance need to be incorporated into the evaluation.

---

# 9. International benchmarking

For cross-country research, it is useful to combine institutional indicators with international datasets and frameworks.

Important sources include:

* **OECD** — digital education, ICT use, student digital skills, education technology
* **UNESCO** — digital learning, technology in education, policy and equity
* **World Bank** — education technology and digital infrastructure
* **European Commission** — Digital Education Action Plan and digital competence frameworks
* **DigCompEdu** — European framework for educators' digital competence
* **SELFIE** — school-level digital capacity self-assessment
* **ITU** — connectivity and digital infrastructure indicators

These frameworks are particularly useful for constructing internationally comparable indicators.

# 10. A stronger research methodology

For a serious academic study, I would recommend combining **four methods** rather than relying on a single index:

### A. Quantitative indicators

Measure:

> infrastructure + adoption + competence + usage + outcomes

### B. Surveys

Survey:

> students + teachers + administrators

### C. Institutional/platform data

Analyze:

> LMS + digital platforms + enrollment + assessment + usage logs

### D. Qualitative research

Conduct:

> interviews + classroom observation + document analysis + case studies

This produces a **mixed-methods evaluation**.

The third level is generally the most important when the research question is about the **degree of digital transformation**, rather than simply ICT penetration.

Yes. For academic research, I would recommend treating the following as a **proposed Digital Education Digitalization Index (DEDI)** rather than claiming that it is an already-established international index. It can be constructed, validated, and then used to compare **China, Japan, Singapore, EU countries, and other education systems**.

The design below combines established international concepts from UNESCO, OECD and the European Commission. UNESCO's framework emphasizes technology as an **input, delivery mechanism, skill, planning tool, and social/cultural context**, while DigCompEdu focuses on educators' digital competence across six areas. # 1. Proposed Digital Education Digitalization Index (DEDI)

I recommend a **0–100 composite index with 6 dimensions and 40 indicators**:

$$
\boxed{
DEDI = \sum_{d=1}^{6} W_dD_d
}
$$

where:

* \(D_d\) = score for dimension \(d\), standardized to 0–100
* \(W_d\) = weight of dimension \(d\)
* \(\sum W_d=1\)

A theoretically balanced starting point is:

| Dimension                                  | Code | Indicators | Initial weight |
| ------------------------------------------ | ---: | ---------: | -------------: |
| Digital infrastructure & access            |  INF |          7 |            15% |
| Digital technology adoption                |  ADP |          7 |            15% |
| Digital pedagogy & learning transformation |  PED |          8 |            20% |
| Digital competence                         |  COM |          7 |            15% |
| Data, AI & intelligent education           |  DAI |          5 |            15% |
| Governance, equity & sustainability        |  GOV |          6 |            20% |
| **Total**                                  |      |     **40** |       **100%** |

These weights are **research starting values**, not assumptions that should automatically be retained. The final study should compare theoretically specified weights with empirically derived weights.

# 2. The 40-indicator framework

## Dimension 1 — Digital Infrastructure & Access (INF)

This measures whether the technological foundations for digital education actually exist.

| Code | Indicator                      | Suggested measurement                  |
| ---- | ------------------------------ | -------------------------------------- |
| INF1 | Internet availability          | % of institutions with Internet        |
| INF2 | High-speed connectivity        | % with broadband/high-speed Internet   |
| INF3 | Network reliability            | Average uptime / perceived reliability |
| INF4 | Student-device availability    | Devices per student                    |
| INF5 | Teacher-device availability    | Devices per teacher                    |
| INF6 | Learning platform availability | LMS/platform availability              |
| INF7 | Home digital access            | % students with adequate home access   |

### Example objective indicators

$$
INF4=\frac{\text{number of usable student devices}}
{\text{number of students}}
$$

and

$$
INF7=\frac{\text{students with adequate home digital access}}
{\text{total students}}\times100
$$

This distinction between **school access and home access** is important because digitalization can otherwise conceal socioeconomic inequalities.

OECD's PISA framework explicitly measures ICT availability and quality of ICT access, including whether schools have enough Internet-connected devices and whether Internet speed is sufficient. ([OECD][2])

---

# 3. Dimension 2 — Digital Technology Adoption (ADP)

Infrastructure tells us whether technology exists. This dimension asks:

> **Is it actually being used?**

| Code | Indicator                               |
| ---- | --------------------------------------- |
| ADP1 | LMS adoption                            |
| ADP2 | Digital learning-material usage         |
| ADP3 | Online assignment usage                 |
| ADP4 | Digital assessment usage                |
| ADP5 | Online collaboration usage              |
| ADP6 | Digital communication usage             |
| ADP7 | Frequency/intensity of digital learning |

For example:

$$
ADP7 =
\frac{\text{digital learning activities per week}}
{\text{total relevant learning activities}}
\times100
$$

This prevents an institution from receiving a high digitalization score simply because it purchased computers.

OECD's recent analysis of PISA 2022 similarly distinguishes **digital infrastructure from actual student and teacher use**, and identifies substantial heterogeneity in how digital resources are used within and across countries. ([OECD][3])

---

# 4. Dimension 3 — Digital Pedagogy & Learning Transformation (PED)

This should receive substantial weight because **digitalization is not synonymous with digitization**.

| Code | Indicator                              |
| ---- | -------------------------------------- |
| PED1 | Blended learning                       |
| PED2 | Flipped learning                       |
| PED3 | Collaborative digital learning         |
| PED4 | Project/problem-based digital learning |
| PED5 | Personalized learning                  |
| PED6 | Digital formative assessment           |
| PED7 | Digital feedback                       |
| PED8 | Student-centered digital learning      |

For example, distinguish:

**Low transformation**

> Teacher uploads a PDF instead of distributing paper.

from:

**High transformation**

> Students collaborate online, receive adaptive feedback, generate digital artifacts, and follow individualized learning pathways.

This distinction is consistent with DigCompEdu's emphasis on using technology to **enhance and innovate education**, rather than merely developing technical skills. ([Joint Research Centre][4])

---

# 5. Dimension 4 — Digital Competence (COM)

This dimension should measure both **teacher and student capability**.

### Teacher competence

| Code | Indicator                     |
| ---- | ----------------------------- |
| COM1 | Basic digital competence      |
| COM2 | Digital content creation      |
| COM3 | Digital pedagogy              |
| COM4 | Digital assessment            |
| COM5 | Learning analytics competence |

### Student competence

| Code | Indicator                                         |
| ---- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| COM6 | Information/media literacy                        |
| COM7 | Digital communication, creation & problem solving |

A more detailed study could split COM6–COM7 into multiple constructs.

The European Commission's DigCompEdu framework is particularly useful here because it contains **22 educator competences across six areas**, including digital resources, teaching and learning, assessment, empowering learners, and facilitating learners' digital competence. ([Joint Research Centre][5])

OECD's PISA 2022 work also distinguishes merely finding digital information from evaluating its quality, which illustrates why "digital skill" should not be reduced to basic device operation. ([OECD][2])

# 6. Dimension 5 — Data, AI & Intelligent Education (DAI)

For a **2026 research instrument**, I strongly recommend adding this dimension.

Traditional ICT indices often predate generative AI and therefore do not adequately distinguish an LMS-based institution from an AI-enabled educational ecosystem.

| Code | Indicator                          |
| ---- | ---------------------------------- |
| DAI1 | Learning analytics                 |
| DAI2 | Adaptive/personalized systems      |
| DAI3 | AI-assisted teaching               |
| DAI4 | AI-assisted student learning       |
| DAI5 | AI literacy and responsible AI use |

For example:

**DAI3**

> Percentage of teachers using AI for lesson preparation, feedback, assessment, tutoring or instructional support.

**DAI5**

could measure whether students and teachers understand:

* hallucinations
* source verification
* privacy
* intellectual property
* bias
* appropriate AI use
* academic integrity

UNESCO's technology-in-education framework already treats technology as a tool for **planning and system management**, including assessment and education-management data. The addition of AI therefore extends rather than replaces the broader digitalization framework. ([2023 GEM Report][6])

# 7. Dimension 6 — Governance, Equity & Sustainability (GOV)

This dimension is critical for cross-country comparison.

| Code | Indicator                                         |
| ---- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| GOV1 | National/institutional digital education strategy |
| GOV2 | Digital governance                                |
| GOV3 | Data privacy/security                             |
| GOV4 | Digital inclusion/equity                          |
| GOV5 | Teacher professional development                  |
| GOV6 | Financial/technical sustainability                |

For example:

### Digital equity

$$
GOV4 = 100-\text{digital access gap}
$$

where the access gap could be measured between:

* urban/rural students
* high-/low-income students
* different regions
* students with/without disabilities
* different school types

UNESCO explicitly emphasizes **access, equity, inclusion, governance, teacher preparation and sustainability** when assessing technology in education. ([UNESCO][7])

# 12. Converting indicators to 0–100

Suppose an indicator is measured on a 1–5 scale.

Use:

$$
X_{0-100} =
\frac{X-1}{5-1}\times100
$$

Therefore:

| Response | Score |
| -------: | ----: |
|        1 |     0 |
|        2 |    25 |
|        3 |    50 |
|        4 |    75 |
|        5 |   100 |

For an objective indicator where higher values represent better performance:

$$
X_i =
100\frac{x_i-\min(x)}
{\max(x)-\min(x)}
$$

For a negative indicator:

$$
X_i =
100\frac{\max(x)-x_i}
{\max(x)-\min(x)}
$$

This makes all indicators point in the same direction.

# 15. Weighting methodology

This is one of the most important methodological questions.

I recommend testing **three weighting schemes**.

## Model A — Equal weighting

$$
w_i=\frac{1}{N}
$$

Advantages:

* transparent
* easy to reproduce
* normatively neutral

Disadvantage:

* assumes every dimension is equally important.

---

## Model B — Expert-derived weighting

Use a panel of experts.

For example:

* education technology researchers
* teachers
* university administrators
* educational policymakers
* instructional designers
* AI/learning analytics specialists

A Delphi study could ask experts to assign importance to the six dimensions.

Then calculate:

$$
W_d=
\frac{\text{mean importance}_d}
{\sum_d\text{mean importance}_d}
$$

This produces theoretically informed weights.

---

# 16. Model C — PCA/FA-derived weighting

For an empirical index, I particularly recommend testing **Principal Component Analysis (PCA)** or **Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA)**.

Suppose PCA produces:

$$
PC_1,\ PC_2,\ PC_3
$$

with explained variances:

$$
42\%,\ 18\%,\ 11\%
$$

Then the components collectively explain:

$$
71\%
$$

of the variance.

Indicator weights can then be derived from factor/component loadings.

However, **PCA should not automatically determine the conceptual structure**. A theoretically designed construct such as digital pedagogy should not disappear simply because it has lower statistical variance.

Therefore:

> **Theory → measurement model → statistical validation → final index**

is preferable to:

> **PCA → whatever factors happen to appear**

---

# 17. Exploratory Factor Analysis

EFA can test whether your proposed six-dimensional structure actually appears in the data.

For example:

$$
X = \Lambda F+\epsilon
$$

where:

* \(X\) = observed questionnaire items
* \(F\) = latent dimensions
* \(\Lambda\) = factor loadings
* \(\epsilon\) = measurement error

You would examine:

* KMO
* Bartlett's test
* factor loadings
* cross-loadings
* communalities
* eigenvalues
* explained variance

A typical criterion might be:

$$
Loading \geq 0.50
$$

although thresholds should be interpreted in context rather than applied mechanically.

---

# 18. Confirmatory Factor Analysis

After EFA, conduct **CFA** on an independent sample.

The hypothesized structure is:

```text
                    Digital Education
                         │
       ┌─────────┬───────┼───────┬─────────┐
       ↓         ↓       ↓       ↓         ↓
      INF       ADP     PED     COM       DAI       GOV
       │         │       │       │         │         │
     items     items   items   items     items     items
```

Important model-fit statistics include:

* CFI
* TLI
* RMSEA
* SRMR
* χ²
* χ²/df

Rather than relying on one threshold, report the full set and justify the criteria.

---

# 19. Reliability testing

For each dimension calculate:

### Cronbach's alpha

$$
\alpha=
\frac{k}{k-1}
\left(
1-\frac{\sum\sigma_i^2}
{\sigma_T^2}
\right)
$$

where \(k\) is the number of items.

A commonly used benchmark is:

$$
\alpha \geq 0.70
$$

But I recommend also reporting **McDonald's omega**, because alpha has important assumptions that may not hold for multidimensional instruments.

---

# 20. Convergent validity

For each latent construct calculate:

### Composite Reliability

$$
CR=
\frac{(\sum\lambda_i)^2}
{(\sum\lambda_i)^2+\sum\theta_i}
$$

and:

### Average Variance Extracted

$$
AVE=
\frac{\sum\lambda_i^2}
{\sum\lambda_i^2+\sum\theta_i}
$$

A commonly used criterion is:

$$
CR>0.70
$$

and

$$
AVE>0.50
$$

---

# 21. Discriminant validity

You need to demonstrate that:

> Digital competence ≠ digital pedagogy ≠ infrastructure ≠ governance.

Otherwise, your six dimensions may simply be measuring one general factor.

Possible approaches include:

* Fornell–Larcker criterion
* HTMT
* cross-loading analysis

I would recommend reporting **HTMT** in addition to traditional methods.

# 22. SEM model

You can then investigate not merely **how digitalized** an education system is, but **how digitalization works**.

For example:

$$
Infrastructure
\rightarrow
Technology\ Adoption
\rightarrow
Digital\ Pedagogy
\rightarrow
Learning\ Outcomes
$$

while:

$$
Digital\ Competence
\rightarrow
Digital\ Pedagogy
$$

and:

$$
Governance
\rightarrow
Technology\ Adoption
$$

You could test:

$$
PED=\beta_1INF+\beta_2ADP+\beta_3COM+\epsilon
$$

and:

$$
OUT=\beta_4PED+\beta_5DAI+\beta_6COM+\epsilon
$$

This turns the project from simply constructing an index into a **theory-testing study**.

# 23. Cross-country comparison: a critical issue

If you compare **China vs Japan vs Singapore vs Europe**, you cannot simply compare raw questionnaire averages.

You need to establish **measurement invariance**.

For example:

```text
China
Japan
Singapore
Germany
France
        ↓
Same questionnaire
        ↓
Configural invariance
        ↓
Metric invariance
        ↓
Scalar invariance
        ↓
Latent mean comparison
```

### Step 1 — Configural invariance

Do the countries have the same factor structure?

### Step 2 — Metric invariance

Are factor loadings comparable?

### Step 3 — Scalar invariance

Are item intercepts comparable?

Only after sufficient invariance is established should you make strong comparisons of latent means.

This is particularly important because a Japanese respondent's interpretation of "often" or "good digital infrastructure" may differ systematically from a Chinese respondent's interpretation.

---

# 24. Multi-group SEM

Once measurement invariance is established, use **Multi-Group Structural Equation Modeling (MG-SEM)**.

For example:

$$
INF\rightarrow ADP\rightarrow PED\rightarrow OUT
$$

can be estimated separately for:

* China
* Japan
* Singapore
* Germany
* etc.

You can then test whether structural relationships differ.

For example:

$$
\beta_{China} \neq \beta_{Japan}
$$

This asks a much more interesting research question than simply:

> "Which country has a higher digitalization score?"

It asks:

> **Do the mechanisms through which digitalization affects education differ across education systems?**

# 27. Avoiding common methodological problems

There are several problems I would explicitly guard against.

### Problem 1 — Infrastructure bias

A country with excellent broadband could score highly even if teachers rarely use technology.

**Solution:** separate INF from ADP and PED.

---

### Problem 2 — Technology quantity ≠ educational transformation

More devices do not necessarily mean better digital education.

**Solution:** give substantial weight to pedagogy, competence and learning processes.

UNESCO specifically cautions that evidence concerning technology's added value in education remains limited and that technology should be evaluated in relation to educational objectives rather than treated as inherently beneficial. ([2023 GEM Report][1])

---

### Problem 3 — Self-report bias

Teachers may overestimate their digital competence.

**Solution:**

$$
Self-report + Platform\ Data + Performance\ Test
$$

---

### Problem 4 — Country cultural bias

The same questionnaire item may behave differently in different countries.

**Solution:** measurement invariance testing.

---

### Problem 5 — AI rapidly changes the measurement environment

An index created around "computer access" can become obsolete.

**Solution:** separate stable dimensions from technology-specific indicators.

For example:

> Digital problem solving

is relatively stable.

> Use of ChatGPT

is technology-specific.

Compare:
